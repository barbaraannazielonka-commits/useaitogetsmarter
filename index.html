Use AI to get smarter, not just faster.

In our first graded assignment, you are allowed to use AI. But I want to show you how to use it so that you become smarter, not dumber. This guide walks you through two simple frameworks that help you use AI to strengthen your thinking, so you can feel proud of your presentation and confident when you deliver it.

The one question that matters

Before you open any AI tool, ask yourself:

✅ Enhance

AI helps me practise, test my understanding, and see what I missed. I still do the learning.

🚫 Bypass

AI does the thinking and I copy the result. I finish the task but I haven't learned a thing.

Why this matters for this task

On presentation day there is no AI in the room. You stand up, speak for up to 10 minutes, and answer questions about your own research question. If you only pasted your understanding from a chatbot, it will disappear the moment someone asks, "Why do you say that?"

The goal: you finish this project able to explain your topic, your sources and your argument without any screen open.

And it goes further than this task. Learning isn't just finishing assignments. It means changing what you can understand and do, so that it stays with you. The presentation ends, but the skills don't: thinking first, asking better questions, checking what you're told, and explaining ideas in your own words. You'll use them in other subjects, in further studies and at work, and in everyday life when you have to judge information about your health, your money or the news. Anyone can get an answer from AI. What makes you capable is being able to understand it, question it and make it your own.

Why your brain needs to do the work

Learning happens when your brain actively engages with new information. When you think, ask questions, connect ideas, solve problems, and learn from mistakes, you strengthen the neural connections that help you understand and remember. This is called productive cognitive effort: the mental work that helps you build knowledge and develop skills. It does not mean making learning unnecessarily difficult. Rather, it means giving your brain the opportunity to do the thinking required for learning.

If you delegate every step to AI, you may produce an impressive presentation, but you miss out on much of the mental work that leads to genuine understanding. Over time, relying too heavily on AI may also contribute to what is increasingly referred to as cognitive debt: a situation in which repeated dependence on technology reduces opportunities to develop and maintain important thinking and problem-solving skills.

Use AI to support your thinking, not to do your thinking for you. Your goal is not simply to complete the task, but to become more knowledgeable, capable, and confident in your own abilities.

Two simple frameworks will help you do that.

Framework 1

Wrestle · Rap · Reflect

Created by Eric Curts. The order is the secret: you go first, AI goes second, and you get the last word.

1 · Wrestle

Struggle with it yourself, without AI

Learning happens when your brain works hard. So start alone. It will feel messy. That's the point.

What you do for your presentation:

  • Write down what you already know about your topic (3–5 bullet points).
  • Write your own first version of your research question, even if it's clumsy.
  • Read at least one source yourself and write two sentences about what it says, in your own words.
  • Write down what confuses you or what you can't figure out yet.

Rule: Don't open AI until you have something on the page. Your "Wrestle" notes go in your OneNote page.

2 · Rap

Talk with AI like a coach, not a ghostwriter

"Rap" means a back-and-forth conversation. Now bring AI in to challenge, question and extend what you've already done.

Show AI your Wrestle notes and ask it to:

  • Ask you questions that reveal gaps in your thinking.
  • Give you counterarguments to go and investigate.
  • Explain a hard idea in simpler words (then you re-explain it back).
  • Quiz you on your topic, one question at a time.
  • Play your tough audience: ask the questions a classmate or teacher might ask.

Rule: Keep the conversation going. If you only send one message and copy the answer, that isn't a "rap," that's a shortcut.

3 · Reflect

Decide what you actually learned and what you'll keep

AI can be wrong, biased, or too confident. Reflection is where you become the judge.

  • What did AI help me understand that I didn't before?
  • What did AI say that I'm not sure is true? How will I check it?
  • What did I not use, and why?
  • Can I now explain my topic out loud without looking? Try it for 60 seconds.
  • What will I change in my presentation because of this?

Rule: Write your reflection in OneNote. It becomes your evidence that you did the thinking.

Framework 2

RAFT: Ethical AI Use

Created by Dr. Chase Cookson. RAFT helps you decide when AI helps, when it crosses the line, and how to keep your evidence, reasoning and voice yours.

Traffic lights for your presentation

🟢 Generally green

  • Search terms to use after your own first attempt
  • Practice questions from your notes
  • Counterarguments to investigate
  • Grammar check that keeps your voice

🟡 Pause and check

  • Summarising material you're expected to understand
  • Rewriting your own work
  • Translating or analysing data
  • Anything where originality is graded

🔴 Generally stop

  • Handing in AI-written work as yours
  • Using citations or quotes you haven't verified
  • Hiding AI use that isn't allowed
  • Letting AI do the central thinking
The RAFT Framework for Ethical AI Use

Use AI without outsourcing your learning

The one questionAm I using AI to enhance my learning, or to bypass it?

Before you begin

  • What am I expected to learn or demonstrate?
  • What AI use is allowed for this assignment?
  • What have I already tried on my own?

Work through RAFT

R

Research Independently First

Gather credible sources. Write down what you know, what you think, and what remains unclear before AI enters the process.

A

Augment Your Work

Ask AI for questions, gaps, counterarguments, feedback, or search directions. Ask for possibilities, not a finished submission.

F

Fact-Check Everything

Find the original trustworthy source. Confirm the exact claim, quotation, date, calculation, and context. Remove what you cannot verify.

T

Transform Into Your Own Work

Close the AI output and build the final product from verified notes, course knowledge, your reasoning, and your voice.

Source: RAFT Framework for Ethical AI Use (Dr. Chase Cookson)

The 60-second RAFT check

  1. Learning: Did I do the thinking this task is meant to assess?
  2. Evidence: Can I verify every claim and every source?
  3. Voice: Does this sound like me and reflect what I understand?
  4. Transparency: Did I follow the AI-use rules and note where I used AI?
  5. Defense: Could I explain this without the AI tool open?

Use the check at three moments: before you ask AI, after you get an answer, and before you rehearse.

⚠️ Important: AI makes up sources

You need 5+ credible sources. AI sometimes invents articles, authors and statistics that sound completely real. Never put a source in your APA list (BibGuru) or say it aloud unless you have opened it and checked it.

Prompts that strengthen learning

A good prompt tells AI its job (coach) and what not to do (don't give me the answer). Tap to copy and replace [brackets].

❌ Weakens learning
"Write a 10-minute presentation about social media and mental health."
✅ Strengthens learning
"Here's what I understand so far: […]. Ask me 3 questions that reveal gaps. Don't give me the answers yet."

Final check before you present

Tick each one honestly. This is for you.

Remember: the best sign you've used AI well is that when it's closed, you still know your stuff. 💪
